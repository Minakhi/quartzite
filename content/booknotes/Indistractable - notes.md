## Define Distraction
  
*For a given time frame*, every action can be classified either as "**traction**" - that which advances your purpose - or "**distraction**" - that which impedes your purpose.  
  
"For a given time frame" is important. A social media notification is a distraction if, for that time frame, your purposeful action is to work. However, if you have decided to spend some downtime on social media, or if your work itself is to be on social media, then the action is "traction" and is advancing your purpose.  
  
  
## Root Causes of Distraction  
  
Understand the root cause - not the proximate cause - of a distraction. The TV remote lying around may be a proximate cause to the distraction of watching TV, but the root cause could be dissatisfaction with you are doing. Or, it could be an unresolved emotional issue that you are trying to hide away from through the comfort of a book, instead of picking up the phone to make the next sale. Removing the proximate causes - the external triggers - might help reduce the instances of distraction, but may not remove the internal insistence on getting distracted - the inner trigger.  
  
Four psychological factors - boredom, negativity bias, rumination, and hedonic adaptation - conspire to make sure we’re never satisfied for long. Evolutionarily, it was a necessity for the progress of the species.  
  
  
## Make yourself Indistractable
  
Four Steps:  
1. Master inner triggers  
2. Make time for traction  
3. Hack back external triggers  
4. Use pacts  
  
---  
  
### 1.  Master inner triggers
  
All motivation is a desire to escape discomfort. If a behavior was previously effective at providing relief, we’re likely to continue using it as a tool to escape discomfort. Anything that stops discomfort is potentially addictive, but that doesn’t make  
it irresistible. If you know the drivers of your behavior, you can take steps to manage them.  
  
For a few days, every day, list out all the occasions when you were distracted. In two columns, list what the "traction task" was, and what you did instead, "distraction task."  
  
Then, write down three root causes of discomfort you may be trying to escape by doing the unwanted behavior. For example, when I was using my phone when I wanted to spend quality time with my daughter, I was trying to escape: 1) the fear that I might be missing out on something important happening at work, 2) the anticipation of a client email, and 3) the boredom that comes from playing with a young child for too long.  
  
Simple abstinence from a temptation may backfire. The temptation builds up and volcanoes out one day. Instead, disarm the temptation by reimagining the task and your temperament.  
  
To find out which temptation to disarm, four steps:  
  
1. Look for the emotion(s) immediately preceding the temptation  
2. Record the internal triggers separately per traction task  
3. Explore the negative sensation with curiosity, not contempt  
4. Be extra cautious during liminal moments. (Liminal moments are “distraction traps” that transition us from one thing to another, like picking up our phone while waiting for a traffic light to change)  
  
Reimagining the task you often label as "boring" as tasks that are "play". Add small challenges, constraints, novelty. It need not be pleasurable; but must feel like a game you're making progress on.  
  
Reimagine your temperament by first avoiding self-defeating self-talk like "I'm not patient", "I have poor self-control". Second, practice self-compassion, instead. Talk to yourself as you'd talk to a friend who is facing the temptation. People who are self-compassionate are more resilient. Remember, we don't run out of will-power. The only way we do is when we think we do.

### 2. Make time for traction
  
To minimise distractions, maximize traction. This can be done by *Time-boxing*, i.e. intentionally setting aside an hour or two hours for a specific "traction" task. For that time frame, any action other than the traction task is a distraction. You can set daily, weekly, and monthly repeatable timeboxes. And you can regularly reflect on and refine your schedules to see what works best for you.  
  
Three life domains for which you have to set traction tasks: you, relationships, work.  
  
#### a. You  
You are the center. If you suffer, everything else does too. So, schedule time for yourself first. Figure out what you value. Then schedule your inputs towards those values. You control only the time you put into something, not the outcome. So, be biased for input. Timebox when you'll put effort towards what value's goal. And then, show up when you said you will.  
  
  
#### b. Relationships  
Your loved ones deserve more than the time left after you have done everything else. Timebox dates with your spouse, partner. Add household chores to your calendar for a more equitable split of responsibilities.  
  
Timebox regular meetings with friends. It is helpful if these are at the same time every week or every other week, since you'll no longer have to coordinate every single time. And it will become a ritual that you all can look forward to. This will deepen your relationships. A lack of deep, meaningful relationships is hazardous to your health.  
  
  
#### c. Work  
Sync your schedule with coworkers. Let them know when you are not available for meetings by telling them you're working on something important that needs your complete presence. Without visibility into your schedule, coworkers will assume they can ask for your time when they need it. If your daily schedules keep changing, do a quick 10-min check-in to align and sync up in the morning.  
  
Take some time to look at your schedule as a game or puzzle. The pieces of the puzzle are the time blocks you’ve now identified in each of your three life domains (you, relationships, work). Much like the game of Tetris, the mission is to arrange and rearrange the pieces of the puzzle in order to fit them into your schedule. Think creatively about how you can make things work. For example, what tasks can you eliminate or outsource? What tasks will you spend less time on? What tasks will you spend more time on? Can you batch similar tasks together?  
  
Eliminate your preconceived notions of what an ordinary day should look like. After all, this is your game, your life, and, therefore, your rules.

### 3. Hack back external triggers  
  
External triggers can be useful too, if you design them to produce traction. First ask yourself, "Is this trigger serving me, or am I serving it?" Eliminate or redesign the ones you are serving into ones that will serve you.  
  
Interruptions lead to mistakes. Open offices are terrible for focused work. Defend your focus with a clear, even outrageous sign that you're indistractable now. Wear a glowing tiara if you have to (Headphones are ambiguous and don't signal indistractability). Also, set up "office hours" or "meeting hours" that people can come at or set appointments for.  
  
  
#### a. Hack back email  
Time spent on email a day (T) equals average number of emails a day (n) multiplied by average time spent on each email (t). T=nt. Minimise both n and t.  
  
Reduce the number of emails received by reducing the number of emails sent. Delay sending emails as long as the other person in willing to wait. Use email schedulers for next morning instead of sending it out in the evening when you're clearing your inbox; this reduces replies at night. Label emails as "today" if they need a response today, or as "this week" if the reply can wait. Timebox an hour later in the week to go through all the "this week" ones. Delete/archive emails that don't need replies. Unsubscribe from newsletters that don't bring you value.  
  
  
#### b. Hack back group chat  
Use real-time chats sparingly. Question company norms - e.g. you're expected to reply to a text immediately - and redefine group chat practices. Don't let group chat/slack be the "always ON" channel. Even Slack itself encourages people to use its DND mode actively. Group chats are great at replacing short meetings when you have to reach a conclusion quickly. Wrap up after the conclusion is reached.  
  
Set expectations with colleagues by letting them know that you have set aside time in your calendar for group chat and, consequently, may not always be available to participate in the discussion. In an effort to stay indistractable, remove group chat’s external triggers by turning on your Do Not Disturb feature within the app until you are ready to participate.  
  
  
#### c. Hack back meetings  
Make it harder to call a meeting: the meeting organizer must send an agenda and briefing document when proposing a meeting. Meetings should be for consensus-building. Barring few exceptions, creative problem-solving should happen individually or in very small groups before bringing it to the larger group.  
  
Be fully present in a meeting. People use devices during meetings to escape monotony and boredom, which subsequently makes meetings even worse. Have only one laptop per meeting - to present, etc. Have everyone send whatever needs to be show to that laptop. At most, have another for the exclusive purpose of taking notes. See if note-taking can be on paper and later digitized after concising.  
  
If you don’t feel comfortable asking your colleagues to try something new,  
experiment with how leaving your own devices at your desk changes your  
participation and concentration during meetings. If they see results, that will make it easier to convince them.  
  
  
#### d. Hack back your smartphone  
This takes less than an hour. Four steps:  
  
**Reclaim**: Turn off all sound notifications except for the most critical. Use badge notifications (red circle on app icon) instead. Use DND to your benefit. Remove push notifications too.  
  
**Remove**: Uninstall apps, widgets that you don't use. You can always reinstall them later if needed.  
  
**Replace**: Move entertainment apps to desktop. Get a wristwatch instead of reaching out to your phone - even the harmless time-check can suck you in if you see a floating notif and also build the habit of turning the phone on.  
  
**Rearrange**: Declutter the home screen. Sort apps into 3 categories:  
	Primary - utility apps with a specific purpose - Gmail, Slack, Uber, Zomato, etc.  
	Aspirations - learning apps that advance your goals - Headspace, Chess, etc  
	Slot Machines - apps that suck you in - Social media, YouTube, Netflix  
  
  
#### e. Hack back your desktop  
Clear the desktop screen. Tuck away icons into folders; use search to fish out what you need. Turn off desktop notifications. Put on a wallpaper that inspires you to focus on traction tasks - the cleaner the better.  
  
  
#### f. Hack back online articles  
Don't read on your browser. Save interesting articles to Pocket - it also removes ads. Listen to articles when working out, folding laundry, or some other autonomous job that doesn't require your full presence ("temptation bundling" - the reward of listening makes the chore bearable)  
  
  
#### g. Hack back your feeds  
Delete social media from your life. Too ascetic? At least kill the infinite scroll. Use free browser extensions like News Feed Eradicator for Facebook, Newsfeed Burner, Open Multiple Websites, and DF Tube to remove distracting external triggers.

### 4. Use pacts  
  
Keeping distractions out is part one. After doing that, use precommitment pacts to rein yourself in.  
  
#### a. Effort Pact  
Make unwanted behaviours difficult to do. Apps like SelfControl, Forest, and Focusmate can help you stay away from other apps till you finish a precommitted traction task. Working next to a colleague or friend for a set period of time can be a highly effective effort pact, if both agree to be each other's accountability partner.  
  
  
#### b. Price Pact  
Burn a 100 rupee note every time you can't do a precommitted task. Caution: use this only after having removed all external triggers. Price Pacts work best when distraction is temporary. Price pacts can be difficult to start. We fear making a price pact because we know we’ll have to actually do the thing we’re scared to do. Learn self-compassion before taking up a price pact. Betting on your own success is shown to be a highly effective commitment  
strategy.  
  
  
#### c. Identity Pact  
Become a noun. By assigning yourself a moniker, you increase the likelihood of following through with behaviors consistent with what you call yourself. Call yourself “indistractable writer". Saying "I'm someone who writes everyday" works better than "I write everyday." An identity pact is a precommitment to a self-image. You can prevent distraction by acting in line with your identity.  
  
Adopt rituals. Repeating mantras, keeping a timeboxed schedule, or performing other routines reinforces your identity and influences your future actions.  
  
Share with others. Teaching others solidifies your commitment, even if you’re still struggling. A great way to be indistractable is to tell friends about what you learned in this book and the changes you’re making in your life.

## Make your Workplace Indistractable
  
Jobs, where employees encounter high expectations and low control, have  
been shown to lead to symptoms of depression. When people feel bad, they use distractions to avoid their pain and regain a sense of control.  
  
Tech overuse at work is a symptom of dysfunctional company culture. More tech use makes the underlying problems worse, perpetuating a “cycle of responsiveness.”  
  
Don’t suffer in silence. A workplace where people can’t talk about technology overuse is also one where people keep other important issues (and insights) to themselves.  
  
Knowing that your voice matters is essential. Teams that foster psychological safety and facilitate regular open discussions about concerns not only have fewer problems with distraction but also have happier employees and customers. Leaders have to exemplify the importance of doing focused work.

## Make your kids indistractable
  
#### a. Avoid convenient excuses  
Stop deflecting blame. When children don’t act the way parents want, it’s natural to look for answers that help parents divert responsibility. Techno-panics are nothing new. From the book, to the radio, to video games, the history of parenting is strewn with moral panic over things supposedly making kids act in strange ways.  
  
Tech isn’t evil. Used in the right way and in the right amounts, kids’ tech use can be beneficial, while too much (or too little) can have slightly harmful effects.  
  
Teach kids to be indistractable. Teaching children how to manage distraction will benefit them throughout their lives.  
  
  
#### b. Understand their internal triggers  
Our kids need psychological nutrients. According to a widely accepted theory of human motivation, all people need three things to thrive: a sense of autonomy, competence, and relatedness.  
  
Distractions satisfy deficiencies. When our kids’ psychological needs are not met in the real world, they go looking for satisfaction—often in virtual environments.  
  
Kids need alternatives. Parents and guardians can take steps to help kids find balance between their online and offline worlds by providing more offline opportunities to find autonomy, competence, and relatedness. e.g., provide more time for unstructured play, give them opportunities to improve at a skill of their choice, etc.  
  
  
#### c. Make room for traction together  
Teach kids about traction and the importance of setting a time for it. If they don't make plans ahead, kids will get distracted.  
  
It’s OK to let your kids fail. Failure is how we learn. Show kids how to adjust their schedules to make time to live up to their values.  
  
Figure out tasks that kids want to do with you or other important people in their lives. Help them timebox those tasks. Introduce an element of play. e.g. On a 100 chits, write 100 tasks you want to do together, and put it all in a jar. On every timeboxed session, pick one chit out of the jar and do it.  
  
  
#### d. Help them with external triggers  
  
Test for readiness. A good measure of a child’s readiness is the ability to manage distraction by using the settings on the device to turn off external triggers.  
  
Kids need sleep. Don't keep TV, video games, screens in their room overnight.  
  
Respect their time. Don't become a distraction. Let them focus on the tasks they have timeboxed for. Don't underestimate their capacity for precommitment. As long as they set the rules, know what is important, even very young kids stick to their timeboxes. Put them in charge, let them monitor, reflect, and refine their behaviour.

## Make your friendships indistractable
  
Interruptions degrade our ability to form close social bonds. We can tackle distraction among friends the same way we beat social smoking, by making it unacceptable to use devices in social situations. Prepare a few tactful phrases—like asking, “Is everything OK?”—to discourage phone usage among friends.  
  
Make time for distraction-free discussions. Select a venue with minimal external triggers to limit distraction (e.g., avoid restaurants with wall-to-wall televisions, bars with loud music).
