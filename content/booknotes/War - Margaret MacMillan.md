## Why should we read this book?

In human history, peace is not the norm. War is. Living in the 21st  century – which has so far been relatively peaceful compared to the previous centuries – our understanding of war is distant and warped, shaped mostly by artistic depictions in movies, books, and social media. This book explores multiple perspectives to complement and balance our experience of large-scale human conflict. 

If we can learn to understand war, maybe we can learn to avoid it. 

## How do we feel about war?

Horror and Dread – from reading about and listening to stories of casualties, human and cultural?

Or is it Excitement and Admiration - fueled by epic scenes in movies and books that glorify gore?

War has been a sticky subject in the human mind. We have made war and then theorized about it every single year of recorded history. War destroys, but also creates, the society as we know it. 

## Are we genetically hardwired for war?

Humans have been wounding and killing each other at least since the later Stone Age. Ask Ötzi, the Iceman mummy discovered in the Swiss Alps. But did we learn violence or were we born hardwired with it?

Chimps and bonobos, our closest genetic relatives, offer differing conclusions. Chimps are war-like: they attack and kill with little to no provocation. Bonobos are much more peaceful: when two strangers meet, they gaze at each other, share food, and hug. So, which are we?

Both? We sure are capable of extreme violence and also extraordinary cooperation. But here’s where we differ: we have a choice. We can choose to go to war over abstract concepts like honour, religion, and nationality. Or, we can choose to domesticate our violent tendencies and work out differences through dialogue. Our centuries of action have cast votes on both sides. 

## So, why do we choose war...?

**Greed**: Mongols warred throughout the 13th and 14th centuries for plunder and loot. Saddam Hussain invaded Kuwait in 1990 for oil. 

**Self-preservation**: Israel preemptively attacked Egypt, Syria, and Jordan in 1967 because it was convinced the three nations were planning a joint aggression.

**Emotions**: Adolf Hitler was afraid Germans had grown weak and submissive after defeat in the First World War. He was angry that Germans didn’t seem to care either. In his head cannon, war could toughen up his countrymen. 

**Ideas**: Holy wars, Democracy vs Dictatorship, Capitalism vs Communism, Us vs Them. And then there are the megalomaniacal ideas of glory – Alexander the Great, and Napoleon. 

## ... and what do we say about it?

**Chivalrous**: In medieval England and Scotland, legends of King Arthur and the Holy Grail, novels of Sir Walter Scott, and similar romantic literature reframed the bloody, violent brutality of wars into ideals of chivalry and heroism. Young men idolized warriors as protectors of men’s rights and women’s honour. 

Honourable: The Roman Republic venerated war. It was mandatory for men to serve 16 years in the military and to complete at least 10 before holding public office. Lavish festivals called Triumphs were organized by the victorious generals for the city from the spoils of war.

**Intelligent**: 6th century China glorified war strategy and battle tactics. Sun Tzu popularized victory without bloodshed. Dynasties used great walls and bribes to deal with invaders, leaving armed conflict as a last resort.

**Innovative**: The [[Roman Empire]] incentivized the improvisation of civil technologies for war. Levered presses used for making grape wine and olive oil were repurposed to build ships, fortifications, and siege weaponry. 

**Cultural**: The Spanish erased the Incas by abducting the Incan Emperor, an act that violated the rules of Incan warfare, but was common military sense in Spain. Without their supreme leader, the heavily hierarchical Incan state rapidly collapsed.  

## But what isn’t modern warfare different?

Modern warfare is  built on three pillars: “Nationalism provided the passion for war, the Industrial Revolution the tools, and the changes in society, the bodies to fight...”

**Nationalism**: After the French Revolution, citizens self-identified as a nation and fought against organized foreign armies like the Prussians at the Battle of Valmy, 1792. It was the first time that the untrained common folk had self-organized around a pride in their geographical identity. And their fighting style was alarming to the professional Prussian soldiers – the French sang songs during battle and fought savagely to death.

**Industrial Revolution**: This brought a surge in innovation and production capacity. Also, it didn’t just mean better and more guns, but also an increased spending capacity of a growing middle- and working-class population. 

**Societal change**: With their increasing importance in manufacturing and economics, for the first time in ages, the common folk felt empowered to participate in discussions about war – something that had been the prerogative only of the elite. 

As a combination of all three factors, wars scaled up to what we today know as “total war”. Armies now numbered in millions, an order of magnitude above what was previously imaginable. And countries had to harness their full economic might to service war efforts. Everyone was involved.

## How do you persuade - or force - unwilling people to fight?

In 18th-century Europe, criminals were offered two choices: be executed or join the army. Poverty is a great motivator too. Even today, the US military often focuses its recruitment efforts in the poorer areas. Status is another. Many cultures expected boys to be warrior-like before they can be called men. They devised status symbols - uniforms with medals, specific hairstyles, legionary standards. 

But joining the army and fighting a war are completely different. Soldiers often have to be trained to continue fighting even when their instincts scream fleeing. In Trotsky’s Red Army, the Soviet Army in the early 20th century, commissars executed retreating soldiers to set an example. Clearly, bravery and courage are not the only things that keep a soldier fighting.

## And what of the civilians who don’t fight?

Civilians have had a checkered history in wars. Sometimes “rules” of war declare that they and their property are to be unharmed. Of course, rules are the first casualties of war anyway. 

Modern warfare, quite coldly, brushes civilian casualty under “collateral damage.” If their side loses, civilians may be killed, enslaved, or deported. 

But some civilians flourish through war. They make money controlling scarce, strategic resources and trading them at steep premiums. Smuggling arms, rations, and refugees is often highly lucrative, albeit at the risk of death. 

Females face a particular torment – rape. Stalin’s soviet soldiers (part of the “good guys” Allied side) raped about 2 million women in a single year in Germany after “liberating” them from Nazis.

At the same time, wars have contributed most to women’s rights, education, and career. Women were trained and recruited to factories and offices to fill in for the men, who’d gone off to fight. In the United Kingdom, women participation in the labour force increased from 23% to 34% during the First World War. Of course, when the men came back from the war, they mistreated the women through workplace harassment, unequal pay, and deliberate sabotage. 

Civilians are also strategic targets. In the American Civil War, General Sherman burned farms and buildings, and stole animals and crops, to cut off a major line of support to the opposing Confederate side.

War doesn’t allow civilians to choose if they want to participate. It calls on them to survive.

## Why do we keep making ‘rules’ of war?

After studying the rules of warfare adopted at the 1907 Hague Conference, Mexican rebel leader Pancho Villa said, “It’s not a game. What is the difference between civilized war and any other kind of war?”

So, what purpose do “rules” actually serve? Aggressors often use rules as an excuse to start war. In 1122 BCE, the duke of Chou invaded the Chinese state of Shang arguing that Heaven had revoked the Shang ruler’s mandate because he was a drunkard and a tyrant. Conveniently, Heaven had given the mandate to the  duke of Chou instead. Even today, “protecting the rules” is a pretty good pretension for launching preemptive strikes, claiming they were preventing a “planned violation” of peace accords.

Sometimes, rules are made by majority consensus to specifically weaken an opponent’s key strength. The US and the UK keep calling biochemical weapons as evil and satanic, but napalm and the atomic bomb seem perfectly okay to them. Less directly, some rules are created to negate enemy tactics. The 1856 Declaration of Paris limited the use of naval blockades: one could no more  create a blockade to seize goods from neutral and opposing ships.

Of course, rules eventually become the first casualty of war. 

## What is the danger of thinking about war in the abstract?

The ancient Greeks dispassionately just catalogued wounds: a spear piercing a groin here, an arrow sticking someone in the eye there. Death was ordinary –⁠ a natural and normal fate for a warrior. But modern day painters, writers, and filmmakers have their own artistic agenda, which often is at odds with the  descriptions of soldiers on the frontlines.

One Soviet woman fighter, for instance, mostly remembers fear. In her very first battle, she says, her heart felt as if it were “about to burst,” and her skin “ready to split.” By contrast, a Canadian general describes the terrible thrill of battle. To him, this adrenaline rush was only made stronger by the knowledge that he could die at any moment.

In art, too, depictions of battle are rich and varied. Some, like the opening scene of Apocalypse Now, a film about the Vietnam War, show the terrible beauty of warfare. Other art, like Francisco Goya’s The Disasters of War, depicts a colorless scene, emphasizing the devastation and destruction of conflict.

When it comes to war, our memories are often selective. The way we think about conflicts is deeply shaped by events that happen after them. Look at how differently we perceive the two world wars of the twentieth century, for example. World War II is seen as a straightforward battle of good versus evil. And that view casts a shadow on our thoughts about World War I, a conflict that seems foolish and immoral in comparison.

What we forget is that many of the soldiers who fought in World War I did believe they’d gone to war for something worthwhile. If nothing else, they were fighting for the safety of their loved ones.

All these differences teach us a valuable lesson. We should be careful about how we represent war and how we think about it. If we paint conflicts with a broad brush, we ignore their complexity. But we mustn’t forget that war can encompass extremes: beauty and horror, wickedness and nobility, destruction and creativity.
